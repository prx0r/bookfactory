## The gold standard: Sanskrit decoded through Trika

For Learn Sanskrit with Abhinavagupta, I would establish one non-negotiable principle:

Every passage must be reconstructed from its smallest meaningful linguistic components, with every transformation explained, before its philosophical significance is interpreted through Trika.

The reader should finish each lesson understanding three distinct things:

1. How Sanskrit produces these exact words. Every root, stem, prefix, suffix, inflection, compound, sandhi transformation and syntactic relationship.
2. What the Sanskrit actually says. A translation demonstrably derived from the grammar, including ambiguities and alternative readings.
3. What the passage means within Trika. Its context in the original work, Abhinavagupta's interpretation where available, Lakshmanjoo's teaching, and how it becomes a living practice.

And we should preserve the approach that makes your slang readings enjoyable: astonishing philosophical ideas, concrete everyday examples, and humour—but without sacrificing philological precision.

### The ten layers of every lesson

| Layer                       | Required output                                                             |
| --------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1. Original witness         | Exact Devanāgarī, IAST, author, work, verse, edition and variants           |
| 2. Recitation               | Syllables, vowel lengths, articulation, metre and audio                     |
| 3. Padaccheda               | Restore the underlying words before external sandhi                         |
| 4. Morphological derivation | Root → affixes → stem → inflected form                                      |
| 5. Compound analysis        | Every member, grammatical relationship and possible interpretation          |
| 6. Sandhi proof             | Exact input → rule → output, with rule identifiers where established        |
| 7. Syntax                   | Case roles, agreement, modifiers, finite and implied verbs                  |
| 8. Translation              | Interlinear gloss → literal translation → readable translation              |
| 9. Trika exegesis           | Source-contextualised metaphysics, commentarial evidence and interpretation |
| 10. Practice and mastery    | Recitation, writing, exercises, memorisation and unseen-text testing        |

The important thing is that these aren't independent encyclopaedia entries. Every layer must build on the preceding one.

### Worked example: Brahmārpaṇam

BHAGAVAD GĪTĀ 4.24 — AS EXPLAINED BY ABHINAVAGUPTA AND SWAMI LAKSHMANJOO

ब्रह्मार्पणं ब्रह्म हविर्ब्रह्माग्नौ ब्रह्मणा हुतम् ।

ब्रह्मैव तेन गन्तव्यं ब्रह्मकर्मसमाधिना ॥

brahmārpaṇaṃ brahma havir brahmāgnau brahmaṇā hutam ।

brahmaiva tena gantavyaṃ brahmakarmasamādhinā ॥

This is an unusually valuable first specimen because we have not only the Gītā itself, but Abhinavagupta's Gītārthasaṅgraha commentary and an explanation of that commentary by Lakshmanjoo.

#### A. First reverse the sandhi

SURFACE FORM

ब्रह्मार्पणम् — brahmārpaṇam

UNDERLYING WORDS

ब्रह्म — brahma — Brahman

अर्पणम् — arpaṇam — Instrument / act of offering

THE TRANSFORMATION

a+a → ā

brahma + arpaṇam → brahmārpaṇam

Two adjacent short a vowels contract to one long ā. This is vowel sandhi, specifically savarṇadīrgha.

That's what I mean by mathematical precision. The reader can reverse the transformation and reproduce it.

But there's another important detail: traditional commentators differ over whether brahmārpaṇam is best analysed here as two words joined by sandhi or a compound describing an oblation whose offering instrument is Brahman. We must record such differences rather than silently choose one parse.

#### B. Then analyse the morphology

| Surface form         | Underlying form       | Exact grammatical analysis                                                                     |
| -------------------- | --------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| brahma               | brahman               | Neuter nominative singular; Brahman                                                            |
| arpaṇam              | arpaṇa                | Neuter nominative singular; instrument or act of offering                                      |
| havir                | havis                 | Neuter nominative singular haviḥ, altered by sandhi; sacrificial oblation                      |
| brahmāgnau           | brahma-agni           | Compound, masculine locative singular; in the fire of Brahman                                  |
| brahmaṇā             | brahman               | Neuter instrumental singular; by Brahman                                                       |
| hutam                | huta                  | Past passive participle, neuter singular; offered                                              |
| brahmaiva            | brahma + eva          | Brahman alone; emphatic eva                                                                    |
| tena                 | tad                   | Instrumental singular, masculine or neuter; by that one/him                                     |
| gantavyam            | √gam                  | Gerundive, neuter singular; to be reached                                                      |
| brahmakarmasamādhinā | brahma-karma-samādhin | Instrumental singular masculine; by one whose concentration is on action understood as Brahman |

Now we go one level deeper, showing the formation of the words themselves, not merely their dictionary definitions.

**Example 1 — हुतम् (hutam)**

√hu + kta → huta

- √hu (हु) — to pour into a sacrificial fire, sacrifice.
- The past passive participle is huta — offered, sacrificed.
- Neuter nominative/accusative singular: hutam.

The grammar establishes that something has been offered. The sacrificial context establishes what kind of offering is intended.

**Example 2 — गन्तव्यम् (gantavyam)**

√gam + tavya → gantavya

- √gam (गम्) — to go, reach, attain.
- -tavya — forms a gerundive expressing what is to be done or undergone.
- gantavya — to be reached or attained.
- gantavyam — neuter singular, agreeing with brahma.

The form gantavya is a morphological formation, not simply a case of ordinary external sandhi.

This matters philosophically: the text says Brahman is what is to be attained, not merely that Brahman is somehow present.

**Example 3 — ब्रह्मकर्मसमाधिना (brahmakarmasamādhinā)**

brahma + karma + samādhinā

Brahman + action + by the one possessing concentration

- brahma — Brahman, the ultimate reality.
- karma — action, from karman, associated with √kṛ, to do.
- samādhi — concentration, collectedness, placing together, formed from sam-ā-√dhā.
- samādhin — one possessing concentration.
- samādhinā — instrumental singular, "by one who is concentrated."

A syntactically supported reading is: "by the one whose concentration is fixed upon action as Brahman."

This is a compound whose internal semantic structure must be established from context and commentary, not guessed from the English meanings of its components.

The ideal book would show formal derivations to this depth for every new construction, with full Pāṇinian rule references where verified, and progressively shorter reminders when a rule has already been mastered.

#### C. Produce the translation from the syntax

The student should now be able to reconstruct the verse.

ANALYTICAL GLOSS

Brahman — offering-instrument — Brahman — oblation\
Brahman-fire (LOC) — Brahman (INSTR) — offered (PPP)\
Brahman — indeed/alone — by him (INSTR) — to-be-attained\
Brahman-action-concentrated-one (INSTR)

RECONSTRUCTED LITERAL TRANSLATION

"The instrument of offering is Brahman; the oblation is Brahman. The oblation is offered by Brahman into the fire of Brahman. Brahman alone is to be attained by the one absorbed in action as Brahman."

No metaphysics has been smuggled into the morphology. We first establish what the language warrants.

#### D. Now bring in Abhinavagupta

Here's where your book becomes fundamentally different from an ordinary grammar.

In his Gītārthasaṅgraha commentary on 4.24, Abhinavagupta interprets the fire as supreme consciousness and the oblation as the manifested universe. The offering is intelligible as a return to the very reality from which manifestation arises. He understands the action of offering in terms of consciousness's own manifestation and illumination.

We can then interpret the Sanskrit through Trika's philosophical framework:

| Offering instrument | The means by which manifestation is offered back to its source       |
| ------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Oblation            | The world of objects and experiences, appearing as consciousness     |
| Fire                | Supreme consciousness, the luminous ground of the offering           |
| Offerer             | Agency recognised as an expression of that same consciousness        |
| Attainment          | Recognition of the reality from which nothing is ultimately separate |

Interpretive mapping, not dictionary definitions of the Sanskrit words.

The extraordinary insight: a sacrifice normally requires distinctions among the sacrificer, the offering, the recipient and the act. Here the verse asserts an ultimate unity beneath those distinctions.

In Trika, this need not mean the world disappears. It means that differentiated manifestation is not outside the one consciousness that manifests it.

And that gives us the crucial philosophical transition:

**Grammatical difference does not entail ultimate ontological separation.**

Sanskrit distinguishes the instrumental (brahmaṇā), locative (brahmāgnau), and subject or complement (brahma, arpaṇam). The verse's metaphysical claim is that these distinct roles can all be understood as Brahman.

That's the bridge between precise grammar and Pratyabhijñā philosophy.

#### E. Then introduce Lakshmanjoo's living interpretation

This is especially useful because Lakshmanjoo actually explains the prayer in the setting of eating.

He describes taking water in the palms before a meal, reciting the verse, and understanding the food, the stomach, the offerer and the act of eating as Śiva's play.

So the lesson ends by reconstructing the ritual:

- Food: the oblation (havis).
- Eating: the act of offering (homa).
- Stomach: the fire into which the offering goes.
- The person eating: the apparent agent, not ultimately separate from Śiva.

The source distinction is essential: the food application is Lakshmanjoo's teaching; the verse itself comes from the Bhagavad Gītā; Abhinavagupta's commentary provides the philosophical interpretation. We do not pretend all three are identical texts.

That is how we'd connect the Sanskrit, the philosophy and the actual morning or mealtime practice without ever sacrificing scholarly integrity.

#### F. The sandhi should work like a mathematical proof

The book should never merely say "sandhi happens here." It must give the exact rule and a reversible demonstration.

For our meal prayer:

| Inputs             | Transformation                                             | Result           |
| ------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------- |
| brahma + arpaṇam   | a + a → ā                                                  | brahmārpaṇam     |
| haviḥ + brahmāgnau | Final visarga from underlying -s becomes r before voiced b | havir brahmāgnau |
| brahma + agnau     | a + a → ā, in a compound                                   | brahmāgnau       |
| brahma + eva       | a + e → ai                                                 | brahmaiva        |

The reader should then perform the inverse exercise: here is brahmaiva; recover the two underlying words and explain the rule that produced the visible form.

That teaches a transferable skill, rather than just memorisation.

### Apply the same treatment to your next prayer

Remember the one you've selected:

अलं भेदानुकथया

alaṃ bhedānukathayā

"Enough of repeatedly discussing difference."

Its internal construction illustrates several different mechanisms.

**Stage 1 — Identify the components**

bheda + anu + kathā

difference + following/recounting + discussion

**Stage 2 — Form the compound**

bheda + anukathā → bhedānukathā

The boundary vowels combine: a + a → ā.

**Stage 3 — Inflect the noun**

bhedānukathā → bhedānukathayā

Feminine ā-stem, instrumental singular, with the ending -ayā.

**Stage 4 — Establish the construction**

alaṃ + instrumental

An idiomatic Sanskrit construction expressing sufficiency or cessation: "Enough of ..."

Then bring in the complete verse, because the second half explains why Abhinavagupta no longer wants to keep discussing difference: the savour of devotion renders even the declaration that everything is one inadequate.

This is also where we would teach the distinction between anu (अनु, following) and aṇu (अणु, minute or limited individual). Sound, spelling, grammar and philosophy reinforce one another, but remain distinguishable.

### Seven editorial rules I'd make compulsory

1. Never invent Sanskrit. Every source passage must be identified by edition, verse and witness. Any correction or reconstruction is visibly marked.
2. Never invent etymologies. A historical derivation, a synchronic grammatical analysis and a Tantric symbolic explanation are three different kinds of claims.
3. Never skip grammatical steps. Every unusual form needs its lemma, inflection, rule and, where relevant, compound tree.
4. Never silently resolve ambiguity. Explain alternative parses and why one is preferred.
5. Never attribute interpretation to the wrong teacher. Distinguish original verse, Abhinavagupta, Kṣemarāja, Yogarāja, Lakshmanjoo and our own synthesis.
6. Never confuse phonemic theology with linguistic grammar. Abhinavagupta's understanding of the Sanskrit alphabet deserves serious treatment, but it isn't a substitute for grammatical derivation.
7. Never present a practice adaptation as an ancient prescription. Distinguish documented ritual instructions from practical suggestions we devise for modern readers.

That needs checking against an edition before publication—not silently normalising either reference.

### What a completed chapter should achieve

I'd use three levels of depth within the same book:

1. **Learn it** — practical entry: Full prayer, pronunciation, natural translation, when to use it. Immediately useful, even if someone reads no further.
2. **Decode it** — rigorous language instruction: Exact phonemes, roots, morphology, compound formation, sandhi proofs, syntax, case relationships and reading exercises.
3. **Recognise it** — Trika exegesis: Textual context, commentary, metaphysical implications, doctrinal disagreements and practical application. The lively philosophical explanations we've been developing become the reward for all that grammatical work.

Then conclude with exercises that require the reader to reconstruct an unfamiliar form, not just repeat the examples.

The definitive test: if a student learns brahmaṇā, they should eventually be able to explain why its form differs from brahma, identify another instrumental singular, and distinguish the grammatical meaning of "by Brahman" from the philosophical claim that the agent is Śiva.

For the publishing pipeline, this suggests a very clear division of responsibility: your `translation-work` corpus supplies the audited Sanskrit and textual witnesses; the book's derivation layer supplies verifiable grammatical proofs; the commentary layer supplies sourced Trika interpretation; and `bookfactory` turns these into a progressive learning experience.

The result is not simply a prayer book with Sanskrit explanations. It's a Sanskrit curriculum in which every newly acquired grammatical skill unlocks another piece of Abhinavagupta's thought, until the student can begin reading his works without depending on a translation.

| Text                            | What we learn                                                     |
| ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Brahmārpaṇam and pocket prayers | Sounds, script and basic compounds                                |
| Śivastotrāvalī                  | Devotion, verbs, pronouns, cases                                  |
| Bhairavastava                   | Nominal compounds, sandhi and metre                               |
| Mahopadeśaviṃśatikā             | Philosophical Sanskrit and syntax                                 |
| Śivasūtras                      | Compact sūtra language and technical vocabulary                   |
| Paramārthasāra                  | Intermediate grammar and metaphysics                              |
| Tantrasāra                      | Extended philosophical prose                                      |
| Parātrīśikāvivaraṇa             | Advanced grammar, phonemic metaphysics and textual interpretation |
| Tantrāloka                      | Advanced independent reading                                      |

Different authors must be identified accurately. It wouldn't all literally be Abhinavagupta.

**Reading mode** — the hilarious, irreverent explanations, grounded in the authentic Sanskrit and its philosophical context.

**Learning mode** — rigorous grammatical decomposition, script instruction, exercises and memorisation. No skipped morphology, invented derivations or approximations presented as exact pronunciation.

The same Sanskrit passage underlies both modes. The curriculum accumulates knowledge: once the instrumental case is learned, later chapters reinforce it rather than re-explaining from zero.

**First volume:** 101 Sanskrit Prayers and Passages for Everyday Recognition — 1–2-line pocket prayers for waking, eating, meditation, work, difficult emotions and sleep; gradually longer verses.

**Achievement standard:** by the end, the student can look at a verse never encountered, identify its grammatical structure, break its compounds apart, reconstruct its sandhi and begin translating it alone.
