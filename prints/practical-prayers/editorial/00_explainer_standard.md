# The prayer explainer: the new canonical editorial contract

## Reader-facing layout, in this order

**THE TEXT** — Complete Sanskrit passage (Devanāgarī + IAST), accurate work/verse reference and attribution. A separated **POCKET LINE** only if a phrase is contiguous, semantically intelligible and witnessed; mark **EXTRACT**, not independently transmitted mantra. Offer the complete verse as optional deeper recitation. For unusually long pieces use an extended-recitation appendix instead of pretending they are instant pocket prayers.

**IN ONE SENTENCE** — The literal proposition/petition the Sanskrit makes. No invented claims or ecstatic prose.

**WHEN** — One concrete moment of the day. Example: before the first bite; at a work transition; before sleep.

**THE ACT** — One small, safe and repeatable devotional action that takes 5–30 seconds for a pocket prayer. Label as *Textual instruction*, *Recorded traditional practice* or *Suggested modern adaptation*. The original text must actually prescribe any action attributed to it.

**THE TURN** — One recognitional insight from the passage, grounded in its syntax and Trika explanation, written as something to contemplate rather than a guaranteed physical event.

**RETURN** — One ordinary action: eat; speak; finish a task; let yourself sleep; seek help. No prescribed warmth, bliss, energy surge, special breathing or “sign of success.”

**SOURCE NOTE** — Original author/verse; commentator and teacher citations; any contested readings or adaptations. A full philological appendix can handle derivations without overloading the practical page.

## Distinguish four different speech acts
- **Mantra:** a traditionally recited formula; do not invent bīja, initiate readers or present edited text as canonical.
- **Prayer:** a request or address (e.g. *astu me*, *bhaveyam*); the petition must be kept grammatically legible.
- **Hymn/description:** praises or describes the Lord; can be contemplated, but do not change it into a request by bad translation.
- **Philosophical claim / practice verse:** an assertion or instruction; may become a contemplative reminder but is not automatically a mantra.

## Three legitimate lengths
**Pocket:** 2–10 words; independently meaningful, explicitly credited as excerpt when applicable. **Verse:** usually 16–32 syllables; keep full as canonical and teach in smaller chunks. **Extended:** long verse or 3+ stanzas; print only if appropriate to section, place in appendix, and give a short editorial memory cue without disguising it as independent mantra.

## Non-negotiable distinction
**Literal Sanskrit → commentarial Trika reading → modern devotional suggestion** are three separate assertions. The book may be poetic and vivid, but cannot cite modern coaching as Abhinavagupta's historical ritual or promise a sensation as scripturally guaranteed. When source wording is disputed, a transparent hold beats a pretty error.

## Exactly what not to write
- “On bite three your chest warms; the goddess has received the offering.”
- “If the skin boundary does not thin, you have failed at aikyabuddhi.”
- “The mantra will remove illness/anxiety.”
- “The verse instructs you to put your hand on your throat” unless the source expressly does.
- “All prayers of this kind are by Abhinavagupta.” Different authors and texts matter.

## Correct tonal model
Warm, lucid, reverent and concrete. A line such as “The senses bring their own flowers. Taste this bite without fighting it.” is closer to the book's aspiration than a dramatic promise of spiritual fireworks.
