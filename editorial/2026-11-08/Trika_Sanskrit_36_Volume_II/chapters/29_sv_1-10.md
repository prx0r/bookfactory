# 29. Three flavours of the path

**Stage 5: Devotion without falsifying the grammar**  
**Utpaladeva · *Śivastotrāvalī* 1.10**  
**EXTRACT — read alongside its complete source verse**

## The Sanskrit

संविन्मार्गोऽयम् आह्लाददुःखमोहैस् त्रिधा स्थितः

*saṃvinmārgo 'yam āhlādaduḥkhamohais tridhā sthitaḥ*

### The complete verse in context

सर्व एव भवल्लाभहेतुर् भक्तिमतां विभो । संविन्मार्गोऽयम् आह्लाददुःखमोहैस् त्रिधा स्थितः ॥

*sarva eva bhavallābhahetur bhaktimatāṃ vibho | saṃvinmārgo ’yam āhlādaduḥkhamohais tridhā sthitaḥ ||*

The larger verse is supplied so that the excerpt never masquerades as an independently transmitted mantra or complete proposition. The lesson analyses the **selected passage** word by word; a later volume can analyse the entire surrounding stanza.

## Before seeing the English

Read the Sanskrit aloud. Cover the translation below. Determine where one word ends, whether any final form has changed through sandhi, and which word is acting as subject or predicate. Ask what the grammar **requires** and what remains open.

**Writing laboratory.** संविन् + मार्ग is an internal compound; the अवग्रह in मार्गोऽयम् marks the following initial a.

## Word-by-word anatomy

| Visible / reconstructed form | Lemma / segmentation | Exact grammatical role | What it can mean |
|---|---|---|---|
| **saṃvinmārgo** | saṃvid/saṃvit + mārga [from saṃvinmārgaḥ] | masculine nominative singular compound | path of awareness |
| **ayam** *(recovered)* | idam | masculine nominative singular demonstrative | this |
| **āhlādaduḥkhamohaiḥ** *(recovered)* | āhlāda + duḥkha + moha | masculine instrumental plural compound | by delight, sorrow and confusion |
| **tridhā** | tri + -dhā | indeclinable adverb | in three ways |
| **sthitaḥ** | √sthā + -ta | masculine nominative singular participle | established, situated |

**Reader’s reconstruction:** **saṃvinmārgo** = path of awareness · **ayam** = this · **āhlādaduḥkhamohaiḥ** = by delight, sorrow and confusion · **tridhā** = in three ways · **sthitaḥ** = established, situated.  Notice how this gives you building blocks rather than an English sentence. Only after identifying the inflections and the omitted or explicit verb should you commit to the literal wording.


## Derivation: reverse it, then rebuild it

1. mārga + ḥ before ayam → mārgo ’yam (aḥ+a yields o and avagraha)
2. āhlāda + duḥkha + moha produces a coordinated compound, instrumental plural -aiḥ
3. tri + -dhā → tridhā (threefold)
4. the internal -n- in saṃvinmārga must be checked against the chosen witness rather than “proven” by a made-up sandhi rule

**What the derivation demonstrates.** Bridges the emotional prayer book with philological care about emotion and religious meaning.

Before moving on, reverse at least one of the displayed transformations without looking. Distinguish an external sandhi rule (changes at a word boundary) from a suffix or inflection rule (formation of an individual word). They are not interchangeable explanations.

## Translation laboratory

**1. A conservative literal reconstruction:** This path of awareness is established threefold through delight, suffering and delusion.

**2. Another defensible rendering:** For devotees, the path of consciousness is lived in three registers: joy, distress and bewilderment.

**3. What the Sanskrit does *not* automatically guarantee:** Tridhā means “threefold”; the Sanskrit does not say that all three emotional modes are equivalently pleasant, or that pain must be rebranded as bliss.

These alternatives are not votes on what to believe. Return to the actual case endings, verb voice and contextual antecedents. If the text permits more than one English formulation, learn what the *Sanskrit* resolves and what the interpretation must supply.

## The Trika turn

Utpaladeva places ordinary emotional life within the path rather than requiring its replacement. The grammatical instrumental plural makes the three modes part of the manner or conditions of the path.

This is a philosophical reading of the passage within nondual Śaivism. Where a doctrine is supplied by the surrounding text or commentarial tradition, it is not a hidden morpheme in the Sanskrit. Grammar determines what is said; philosophy asks what follows.
