# 32. Beyond the polarity of pleasure and pain

## The Sanskrit

न दुःखं न सुखं यत्र न ग्राह्यं ग्राहकं न च

*na duḥkhaṃ na sukhaṃ yatra na grāhyaṃ grāhakaṃ na ca*

### The complete verse in context

न दुःखं न सुखं यत्र न ग्राह्यं ग्राहकं न च । न चास्ति मूढभावोऽपि तद् अस्ति परमार्थतः ॥

*na duḥkhaṃ na sukhaṃ yatra na grāhyaṃ grāhakaṃ na ca | na cāsti mūḍhabhāvo ’pi tad asti paramārthataḥ ||*

The larger verse is supplied so that the excerpt never masquerades as an independently transmitted mantra or complete proposition. The lesson analyses the **selected passage** word by word; a later volume can analyse the entire surrounding stanza.

## Before seeing the English

Read the Sanskrit aloud. Cover the translation below. Determine where one word ends, whether any final form has changed through sandhi, and which word is acting as subject or predicate. Ask what the grammar **requires** and what remains open.

**Writing laboratory.** ग्राह्यम्: ह्य = ह् + य; ग्राहकं uses क instead. दुःखं contains visarga-like sign inside word and anusvāra at end.

## Word-by-word anatomy

| Visible / reconstructed form | Lemma / segmentation | Exact grammatical role | What it can mean |
|---|---|---|---|
| **na** | na | indeclinable negative, repeated | not, neither |
| **duḥkham** *(recovered)* | duḥkha | neuter nominative singular | pain, suffering |
| **sukham** *(recovered)* | sukha | neuter nominative singular | pleasure, ease |
| **yatra** | yatra | relative indeclinable | where |
| **grāhyam** *(recovered)* | grāhya (√grah gerundive) | neuter nominative singular | what is grasped/known, object |
| **grāhakam** *(recovered)* | grāhaka | neuter nominative singular in this contrast | grasper/knower, subject-role |
| **ca** | ca | coordinating particle | and |

**Reader’s reconstruction:** **na** = not, neither · **duḥkham** = pain, suffering · **sukham** = pleasure, ease · **yatra** = where · **grāhyam** = what is grasped/known, object · **grāhakam** = grasper/knower, subject-role · **ca** = and.  Notice how this gives you building blocks rather than an English sentence. Only after identifying the inflections and the omitted or explicit verb should you commit to the literal wording.


## Derivation: reverse it, then rebuild it

1. √grah + -ya → grāhya (to-be-grasped, passive potential adjective)
2. grāhaka from √grah with agent-forming suffix -aka
3. contrast grāhya versus grāhaka
4. the second half supplies asti (“exists”), absent in the selected first half

**What the derivation demonstrates.** The argument deepens: ultimate nonduality is not the suppression of experience but questioning its presumed segmentation.

Before moving on, reverse at least one of the displayed transformations without looking. Distinguish an external sandhi rule (changes at a word boundary) from a suffix or inflection rule (formation of an individual word). They are not interchangeable explanations.

## Translation laboratory

**1. A conservative literal reconstruction:** Where there is neither pain nor pleasure, neither object nor subject…

**2. Another defensible rendering:** Where pleasure and pain, what is grasped and what grasps, no longer divide the field…

**3. What the Sanskrit does *not* automatically guarantee:** The negations concern ultimate analysis and do not entail bodily anaesthesia or an inability to distinguish ordinary subjects and objects. The first half is grammatically completed by the second.

These alternatives are not votes on what to believe. Return to the actual case endings, verb voice and contextual antecedents. If the text permits more than one English formulation, learn what the *Sanskrit* resolves and what the interpretation must supply.

## The Trika turn

The paired participial forms grāhya and grāhaka allow the reader to feel a philosophical contrast in suffixes: to-be-known versus knower. The theological reading must be downstream of that morphology.

This is a philosophical reading of the passage within nondual Śaivism. Where a doctrine is supplied by the surrounding text or commentarial tradition, it is not a hidden morpheme in the Sanskrit. Grammar determines what is said; philosophy asks what follows.
