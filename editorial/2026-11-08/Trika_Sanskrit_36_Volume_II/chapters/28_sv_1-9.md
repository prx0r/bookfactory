# 28. May devotion never be separated

**Stage 5: Devotion without falsifying the grammar**  
**Utpaladeva · *Śivastotrāvalī* 1.9**  
**EXTRACT — read alongside its complete source verse**

## The Sanskrit

अवियुक्तास्ति ते तद्वद् एका त्वद्भक्तिर् अस्तु मे

*aviyuktāsti te tadvad ekā tvadbhaktir astu me*

### The complete verse in context

अनन्तानन्दसरसी देवी प्रियतमा यथा । अवियुक्तास्ति ते तद्वद् एका त्वद्भक्तिर् अस्तु मे ॥

*anantānandasarasī devī priyatamā yathā | aviyuktāsti te tadvad ekā tvadbhaktir astu me ||*

The larger verse is supplied so that the excerpt never masquerades as an independently transmitted mantra or complete proposition. The lesson analyses the **selected passage** word by word; a later volume can analyse the entire surrounding stanza.

## Before seeing the English

Read the Sanskrit aloud. Cover the translation below. Determine where one word ends, whether any final form has changed through sandhi, and which word is acting as subject or predicate. Ask what the grammar **requires** and what remains open.

**Writing laboratory.** अस्ति and अस्तु differ by one vowel sign; भक्तिरस्तु writes r from भक्ति: + अस्तु sandhi.

## Word-by-word anatomy

| Visible / reconstructed form | Lemma / segmentation | Exact grammatical role | What it can mean |
|---|---|---|---|
| **aviyuktā** | a- + vi-√yuj + -ta | feminine nominative singular | not separated |
| **asti** *(recovered)* | √as | present third singular | is |
| **te** | tvad | genitive/dative singular enclitic | yours / to you |
| **tadvat** *(recovered)* | tadvat | indeclinable | in the same way |
| **ekā** | eka | feminine nominative singular | one, single |
| **tvadbhaktir** | tvad + bhakti | feminine nominative singular | devotion to you |
| **astu** | √as | imperative third singular | let there be |
| **me** | mad | genitive/dative singular enclitic | mine / for me |

**Reader’s reconstruction:** **aviyuktā** = not separated · **asti** = is · **te** = yours · **tadvat** = in the same way · **ekā** = one, single · **tvadbhaktir** = devotion to you · **astu** = let there be · **me** = mine.  Notice how this gives you building blocks rather than an English sentence. Only after identifying the inflections and the omitted or explicit verb should you commit to the literal wording.


## Derivation: reverse it, then rebuild it

1. a- + viyuktā → aviyuktā
2. aviyuktā + asti → aviyuktāsti (ā + a → ā)
3. bhaktiḥ + astu → bhaktir astu (iḥ before vowel becomes ir)
4. √as present asti versus imperative astu differ in final vowel

**What the derivation demonstrates.** A finely shaped lesson in verbal mood, complete-verse context and the meaning of a devotional wish.

Before moving on, reverse at least one of the displayed transformations without looking. Distinguish an external sandhi rule (changes at a word boundary) from a suffix or inflection rule (formation of an individual word). They are not interchangeable explanations.

## Translation laboratory

**1. A conservative literal reconstruction:** Just so, may my single devotion to You exist, as [Your goddess] is inseparable from You.

**2. Another defensible rendering:** May devotion to You be as inseparable from me as Your beloved goddess is from You.

**3. What the Sanskrit does *not* automatically guarantee:** The first half of the verse gives the Goddess as the simile; without it, aviyuktā appears without an overtly stated feminine subject. Keep the full stanza visible.

These alternatives are not votes on what to believe. Return to the actual case endings, verb voice and contextual antecedents. If the text permits more than one English formulation, learn what the *Sanskrit* resolves and what the interpretation must supply.

## The Trika turn

An explicit prayer enters late in the curriculum. The reader can distinguish statement asti (“is”) from wish astu (“let it be”). The devotional request is not a logical denial of separation; it is a lived petition.

This is a philosophical reading of the passage within nondual Śaivism. Where a doctrine is supplied by the surrounding text or commentarial tradition, it is not a hidden morpheme in the Sanskrit. Grammar determines what is said; philosophy asks what follows.
