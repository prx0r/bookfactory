# 26. Two identical sounds, two different pronouns

**Stage 5: Devotion without falsifying the grammar**  
**Utpaladeva · *Śivastotrāvalī* 1.6**  
**EXTRACT — read alongside its complete source verse**

## The Sanskrit

अनन्तानन्दसिन्धोस्ते नाथ तत्त्वं विदन्ति ते

*anantānandasindhos te nātha tattvaṃ vidanti te*

### The complete verse in context

अनन्तानन्दसिन्धोस्ते नाथ तत्त्वं विदन्ति ते । तादृश एव ये सान्द्रभक्त्यानन्दरसाप्लुताः ॥

*anantānandasindhos te nātha tattvaṃ vidanti te | tādṛśa eva ye sāndrabhaktyānandarasāplutāḥ ||*

The larger verse is supplied so that the excerpt never masquerades as an independently transmitted mantra or complete proposition. The lesson analyses the **selected passage** word by word; a later volume can analyse the entire surrounding stanza.

## Before seeing the English

Read the Sanskrit aloud. Cover the translation below. Determine where one word ends, whether any final form has changed through sandhi, and which word is acting as subject or predicate. Ask what the grammar **requires** and what remains open.

**Writing laboratory.** अनन्तानन्द contains two long ā vowels; सिन्धोः ends with visarga; वेद? Here the actual verb is विदन्ति with short i.

These are **orthographic reading units** to help you construct Devanāgarī, not a metrical scansion. When a consonant ends a word without an inherent vowel, notice its virāma. Trace the conjuncts rather than inserting English-style epenthetic vowels.

Write the selected IAST in Devanāgarī using the reference sheet. Check consonants, vowel lengths and the presence of anusvāra (ं), visarga (ः) and virāma (्). It is fine to consult the chart; the point is a repeatable skill, not instant recognition.

## Word-by-word anatomy

| Visible / reconstructed form | Lemma / segmentation | Exact grammatical role | What it can mean |
|---|---|---|---|
| **anantānandasindhos** | ananta + ānanda + sindhu [from anantānandasindhoḥ] | masculine genitive singular compound | of the ocean of endless bliss |
| **te¹** | tvad | genitive singular enclitic | your |
| **nātha** | nātha | masculine vocative singular | O Lord |
| **tattvam** *(recovered)* | tattva | neuter accusative singular | reality, essential truth |
| **vidanti** | √vid | present active third plural | know |
| **te²** | tad | masculine nominative plural | they, those |

**Reader’s reconstruction:** **anantānandasindhos** = of the ocean of endless bliss · **te¹** = your · **nātha** = O Lord · **tattvam** = reality, essential truth · **vidanti** = know · **te²** = they, those.  Notice how this gives you building blocks rather than an English sentence. Only after identifying the inflections and the omitted or explicit verb should you commit to the literal wording.


## Derivation: reverse it, then rebuild it

1. ananta + ānanda → anantānanda (a+ā→ā)
2. sindhu + gen.sg -oḥ → sindhoḥ
3. sindhoḥ + te → sindhos te (visarga before dental t becomes s)
4. vid + 3pl present → vidanti (not vi + danti)
5. te¹ and te² share spelling but come from different pronouns

**What the derivation demonstrates.** The best demonstration in the entire volume of why case, not intuition, determines meaning.

Before moving on, reverse at least one of the displayed transformations without looking. Distinguish an external sandhi rule (changes at a word boundary) from a suffix or inflection rule (formation of an individual word). They are not interchangeable explanations.

## Translation laboratory

**1. A conservative literal reconstruction:** They know your reality, O Lord, of you who are an ocean of infinite bliss.

**2. Another defensible rendering:** Those immersed in the bliss of devotion know what You truly are, O Lord.

**3. What the Sanskrit does *not* automatically guarantee:** The two te forms **do not** mean the same thing. First “your,” second “they.” The following relative ye... identifies who “they” are. A sentence translation without that context is structurally incomplete.

These alternatives are not votes on what to believe. Return to the actual case endings, verb voice and contextual antecedents. If the text permits more than one English formulation, learn what the *Sanskrit* resolves and what the interpretation must supply.

## The Trika turn

This is the exact point of the book: the student sees identical phonetic strings, discovers distinct morphological functions and stops trusting an English line alone.

This is a philosophical reading of the passage within nondual Śaivism. Where a doctrine is supplied by the surrounding text or commentarial tradition, it is not a hidden morpheme in the Sanskrit. Grammar determines what is said; philosophy asks what follows.
