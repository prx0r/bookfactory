# 23. The thirty-sixfold world appears

**Stage 4: The world in the sentence**  
**Abhinavagupta · *Paramārthasāra* 11**  
**EXTRACT — read alongside its complete source verse**

## The Sanskrit

यत् परतत्त्वं तस्मिन् विभाति षट्त्रिंशदात्म जगत्

*yat paratattvaṃ tasmin vibhāti ṣaṭtriṃśadātma jagat*

### The complete verse in context

सर्वविकल्पविहीनं शुद्धं शान्तं लयोदयविहीनम् । यत् परतत्त्वं तस्मिन् विभाति षट्त्रिंशदात्म जगत् ॥

*sarvavikalpavihīnaṃ śuddhaṃ śāntaṃ layodayavihīnam | yat paratattvaṃ tasmin vibhāti ṣaṭtriṃśadātma jagat ||*

The larger verse is supplied so that the excerpt never masquerades as an independently transmitted mantra or complete proposition. The lesson analyses the **selected passage** word by word; a later volume can analyse the entire surrounding stanza.

## Before seeing the English

Read the Sanskrit aloud. Cover the translation below. Determine where one word ends, whether any final form has changed through sandhi, and which word is acting as subject or predicate. Ask what the grammar **requires** and what remains open.

**Writing laboratory.** षट् = ṣaṭ with final dental? ट retroflex; त्रिंशत् = triṃśat; the visible त्म cluster in आत्मा recurs inside षट्त्रिंशदात्म.

## Word-by-word anatomy

| Visible / reconstructed form | Lemma / segmentation | Exact grammatical role | What it can mean |
|---|---|---|---|
| **yat** | yad | neuter relative pronoun nominative singular | which |
| **paratattvam** *(recovered)* | para + tattva | neuter nominative singular | supreme reality |
| **tasmin** | tad | neuter locative singular | in that |
| **vibhāti** | vi-√bhā | present active third singular | shines, appears distinctly |
| **ṣaṭtriṃśadātma** | ṣaṭ + triṃśat + ātman | neuter nominative singular compound adjective | having thirty-six as its nature |
| **jagat** | jagat | neuter nominative singular | world, moving universe |

**Reader’s reconstruction:** **yat** = which · **paratattvam** = supreme reality · **tasmin** = in that · **vibhāti** = shines, appears distinctly · **ṣaṭtriṃśadātma** = having thirty-six as its nature · **jagat** = world, moving universe.  Notice how this gives you building blocks rather than an English sentence. Only after identifying the inflections and the omitted or explicit verb should you commit to the literal wording.


## Derivation: reverse it, then rebuild it

1. para + tattva → paratattva
2. tad → tasmin (neuter loc.sg)
3. vi + bhāti → vibhāti (preverb + verb)
4. ṣaṭ + triṃśat = thirty-six, then + ātman as compound-final
5. “ṣaṭtriṃśadātma” is an inflected compound form, not a free-standing word for “tattvas”

**What the derivation demonstrates.** Case grammar here *is* metaphysical structure: in what does the world appear?

Before moving on, reverse at least one of the displayed transformations without looking. Distinguish an external sandhi rule (changes at a word boundary) from a suffix or inflection rule (formation of an individual word). They are not interchangeable explanations.

## Translation laboratory

**1. A conservative literal reconstruction:** The world, whose nature is thirty-sixfold, shines in that supreme reality.

**2. Another defensible rendering:** Within the supreme principle, the universe appears as a thirty-sixfold articulation.

**3. What the Sanskrit does *not* automatically guarantee:** The whole verse first describes that reality as free from alternatives and arising/ceasing. “Thirty-six tattvas” is a very plausible Trika contextual gloss, but the phrase literally means “thirty-six-natured.”

These alternatives are not votes on what to believe. Return to the actual case endings, verb voice and contextual antecedents. If the text permits more than one English formulation, learn what the *Sanskrit* resolves and what the interpretation must supply.

## The Trika turn

Syntax gives a containment claim: the thirty-sixfold world appears *in that* (tasmin), not as an independent realm outside it. This is one of the most powerful reversals of the sense of separation.

This is a philosophical reading of the passage within nondual Śaivism. Where a doctrine is supplied by the surrounding text or commentarial tradition, it is not a hidden morpheme in the Sanskrit. Grammar determines what is said; philosophy asks what follows.
