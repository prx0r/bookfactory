# 35. How the infinite becomes the finite

**Stage 6: What persists?**  
**Abhinavagupta · *Paramārthasāra* 16**  
**EXTRACT — read alongside its complete source verse**

## The Sanskrit

मायापरिग्रहवशाद् बोधो मलिनः पुमान् पशुर् भवति

*māyāparigrahavaśād bodho malinaḥ pumān paśur bhavati*

### The complete verse in context

मायापरिग्रहवशाद् बोधो मलिनः पुमान् पशुर् भवति । कालकलानियतिबलाद् रागाविद्यावशेन संबद्धः ॥

*māyāparigrahavaśād bodho malinaḥ pumān paśur bhavati | kālakalāniyatibalād rāgāvidyāvaśena saṃbaddhaḥ ||*

The larger verse is supplied so that the excerpt never masquerades as an independently transmitted mantra or complete proposition. The lesson analyses the **selected passage** word by word; a later volume can analyse the entire surrounding stanza.

## Before seeing the English

Read the Sanskrit aloud. Cover the translation below. Determine where one word ends, whether any final form has changed through sandhi, and which word is acting as subject or predicate. Ask what the grammar **requires** and what remains open.

**Writing laboratory.** मायापरिग्रह has ग्रह conjunct; मलिनः ends in visarga; पुमान् a long-ā nominative; पशुर् has explicit r in sandhi.

## Word-by-word anatomy

| Visible / reconstructed form | Lemma / segmentation | Exact grammatical role | What it can mean |
|---|---|---|---|
| **māyāparigrahavaśād** | māyā + parigraha + vaśa [from māyāparigrahavaśāt] | masculine ablative singular compound | because of coming under the sway of adopting Māyā |
| **bodho** | bodha | masculine nominative singular | awareness/cognition |
| **malinaḥ** | malina | masculine nominative singular adjective | soiled, obscured |
| **pumān** | puṃs | masculine nominative singular | person, individual |
| **paśur** | paśu | masculine nominative singular | bound subject (technical Śaiva sense) |
| **bhavati** | √bhū | present active third singular | becomes, comes to be |

**Reader’s reconstruction:** **māyāparigrahavaśād** = because of coming under the sway of adopting Māyā · **bodho** = awareness/cognition · **malinaḥ** = soiled, obscured · **pumān** = person, individual · **paśur** = bound subject (technical Śaiva sense) · **bhavati** = becomes, comes to be.  Notice how this gives you building blocks rather than an English sentence. Only after identifying the inflections and the omitted or explicit verb should you commit to the literal wording.


## Derivation: reverse it, then rebuild it

1. māyā-parigraha-vaśa → ablative -āt
2. final -t before voiced b becomes -d
3. bodhaḥ + malinaḥ → bodho malinaḥ (aḥ before m→o)
4. paśuḥ + bhavati → paśur bhavati (-uḥ→ur before voiced b)
5. √bhū present third singular gives bhavati, a present stem formation

**What the derivation demonstrates.** All earlier morphology returns in a single powerful statement about limitation.

Before moving on, reverse at least one of the displayed transformations without looking. Distinguish an external sandhi rule (changes at a word boundary) from a suffix or inflection rule (formation of an individual word). They are not interchangeable explanations.

## Translation laboratory

**1. A conservative literal reconstruction:** Through the sway of adopting Māyā, awareness, obscured, becomes the limited person, a bound subject.

**2. Another defensible rendering:** In taking on Māyā, knowing appears contracted as a finite experiencer—a paśu.

**3. What the Sanskrit does *not* automatically guarantee:** The subject chain is compressed and warrants discussion: bodhaḥ, pumān and paśuḥ are all nominatives, while malinaḥ qualifies the state of the subject. Avoid inventing three separate substances merely because English wants separate subjects.

These alternatives are not votes on what to believe. Return to the actual case endings, verb voice and contextual antecedents. If the text permits more than one English formulation, learn what the *Sanskrit* resolves and what the interpretation must supply.

## The Trika turn

We can now reconstruct the contraction argument without mythology. A causal ablative gives a condition; a finite verb names becoming; nominal forms identify what has become bound. Recognition will reverse mistaken identification without pretending individual embodiment was merely linguistic.

This is a philosophical reading of the passage within nondual Śaivism. Where a doctrine is supplied by the surrounding text or commentarial tradition, it is not a hidden morpheme in the Sanskrit. Grammar determines what is said; philosophy asks what follows.
