# 31. The “I” that feels happy and sad

**Stage 6: What persists?**  
**Kallaṭa / Vasugupta tradition; exact authorship debated · *Spandakārikā* 1.4**  
**EXTRACT — read alongside its complete source verse**

## The Sanskrit

अहं सुखी च दुःखी च रक्तश्चेत्यादिसंविदः

*ahaṃ sukhī ca duḥkhī ca raktaś cetyādisaṃvidaḥ*

### The complete verse in context

अहं सुखी च दुःखी च रक्तश्चेत्यादिसंविदः । सुखाद्यवस्थानुस्यूते वर्तन्तेऽन्यत्र ताः स्फुटम् ॥

*ahaṃ sukhī ca duḥkhī ca raktaś cetyādisaṃvidaḥ | sukhādyavasthānusyūte vartante ’nyatra tāḥ sphuṭam ||*

The larger verse is supplied so that the excerpt never masquerades as an independently transmitted mantra or complete proposition. The lesson analyses the **selected passage** word by word; a later volume can analyse the entire surrounding stanza.

## Before seeing the English

Read the Sanskrit aloud. Cover the translation below. Determine where one word ends, whether any final form has changed through sandhi, and which word is acting as subject or predicate. Ask what the grammar **requires** and what remains open.

**Writing laboratory.** रक्तश्चे: रक् + त, then च्; चेत्य writes त्य. सुखी and दुःखी both long ी.

## Word-by-word anatomy

| Visible / reconstructed form | Lemma / segmentation | Exact grammatical role | What it can mean |
|---|---|---|---|
| **ahaṃ** | asmad | first-person nominative singular | I |
| **sukhī** | sukhin | masculine nominative singular | happy, possessed of pleasure |
| **ca** | ca | conjunction | and |
| **duḥkhī** | duḥkhin | masculine nominative singular | suffering |
| **raktaś** | √rañj + -ta | masculine nominative singular | coloured, attached, impassioned |
| **cetyādisaṃvidaḥ** | iti + ādi + saṃvid | feminine nominative plural compound | cognitions such as “thus [I am]…” |

**Reader’s reconstruction:** **ahaṃ** = I · **sukhī** = happy, possessed of pleasure · **ca** = and · **duḥkhī** = suffering · **raktaś** = coloured, attached, impassioned · **cetyādisaṃvidaḥ** = cognitions such as “thus [I am]…”.  Notice how this gives you building blocks rather than an English sentence. Only after identifying the inflections and the omitted or explicit verb should you commit to the literal wording.


## Derivation: reverse it, then rebuild it

1. raktaḥ + ca → raktaś ca (visarga becomes ś before palatal c)
2. ca + iti → ceti (a+i→e)
3. ceti + ādi → cetyādi (i+ā→yā)
4. saṃvid feminine nominative plural is saṃvidaḥ, not a masculine singular ending
5. sukhin → sukhī, duḥkhin → duḥkhī

**What the derivation demonstrates.** A practical demonstration of the difference between the content of a self-description and the knowing of that content.

Before moving on, reverse at least one of the displayed transformations without looking. Distinguish an external sandhi rule (changes at a word boundary) from a suffix or inflection rule (formation of an individual word). They are not interchangeable explanations.

## Translation laboratory

**1. A conservative literal reconstruction:** Experiences such as “I am happy, I am sad, I am attached”…

**2. Another defensible rendering:** The cognitions “I am happy,” “I am distressed,” “I am in love,” and the like…

**3. What the Sanskrit does *not* automatically guarantee:** This is a plural **subject phrase**, not a standalone assertion about what the “I” essentially is. The next half says these cognitions appear in another principle through the succession of states.

These alternatives are not votes on what to believe. Return to the actual case endings, verb voice and contextual antecedents. If the text permits more than one English formulation, learn what the *Sanskrit* resolves and what the interpretation must supply.

## The Trika turn

Notice that happiness and sadness are first introduced as predications (“I am ...”), then reclassified as cognitions. The syntax begins the inquiry into what persists across changing mental states.

This is a philosophical reading of the passage within nondual Śaivism. Where a doctrine is supplied by the surrounding text or commentarial tradition, it is not a hidden morpheme in the Sanskrit. Grammar determines what is said; philosophy asks what follows.
