# 24. The impossible made possible

**Stage 4: The world in the sentence**  
**Abhinavagupta · *Paramārthasāra* 14**  
**EXTRACT — read alongside its complete source verse**

## The Sanskrit

परमं यत् स्वातन्त्र्यं दुर्घटसंपादनं महेशस्य

*paramaṃ yat svātantryaṃ durghaṭasaṃpādanaṃ maheśasya*

### The complete verse in context

परमं यत् स्वातन्त्र्यं दुर्घटसंपादनं महेशस्य । देवी मायाशक्तिः स्वात्मावरणं शिवस्य ॥

*paramaṃ yat svātantryaṃ durghaṭasaṃpādanaṃ maheśasya | devī māyāśaktiḥ svātmāvaraṇaṃ śivasya ||*

The larger verse is supplied so that the excerpt never masquerades as an independently transmitted mantra or complete proposition. The lesson analyses the **selected passage** word by word; a later volume can analyse the entire surrounding stanza.

## Before seeing the English

Read the Sanskrit aloud. Cover the translation below. Determine where one word ends, whether any final form has changed through sandhi, and which word is acting as subject or predicate. Ask what the grammar **requires** and what remains open.

**Writing laboratory.** दुर्घट has र्घ conjunct; सम्पादन uses म्प; स्वतन्त्र्य and महेशस्य have multiple conjunct patterns.

## Word-by-word anatomy

| Visible / reconstructed form | Lemma / segmentation | Exact grammatical role | What it can mean |
|---|---|---|---|
| **paramam** *(recovered)* | parama | neuter nominative singular | supreme |
| **yat** | yad | neuter relative nominative singular | which |
| **svātantryam** *(recovered)* | svatantra + abstract -ya | neuter nominative singular | absolute autonomy, freedom |
| **durghaṭasaṃpādanam** *(recovered)* | dur-ghaṭa + saṃpādana | neuter nominative singular compound | bringing about what is hard/impossible to bring about |
| **maheśasya** | mahā + īśa → maheśa | masculine genitive singular | of the Great Lord |

**Reader’s reconstruction:** **paramam** = supreme · **yat** = which · **svātantryam** = absolute autonomy, freedom · **durghaṭasaṃpādanam** = bringing about what is hard/impossible to bring about · **maheśasya** = of the Great Lord.  Notice how this gives you building blocks rather than an English sentence. Only after identifying the inflections and the omitted or explicit verb should you commit to the literal wording.


## Derivation: reverse it, then rebuild it

1. dur- + ghaṭa yields durghaṭa (hard to effect)
2. saṃ-√pad + -ana yields saṃpādana (accomplishment)
3. mahat/mahā + īśa produces maheśa as a standard compound
4. maheśa + -sya → maheśasya

**What the derivation demonstrates.** An advanced lesson in respecting an unfinished Sanskrit clause even when it would make a wonderful standalone slogan.

Before moving on, reverse at least one of the displayed transformations without looking. Distinguish an external sandhi rule (changes at a word boundary) from a suffix or inflection rule (formation of an individual word). They are not interchangeable explanations.

## Translation laboratory

**1. A conservative literal reconstruction:** Which supreme freedom of the Great Lord accomplishes what is difficult to accomplish…

**2. Another defensible rendering:** The Lord’s sovereignty, capable of bringing the apparently impossible into manifestation, is the subject of this definition.

**3. What the Sanskrit does *not* automatically guarantee:** This selected first half is not a complete assertion: the following half identifies the goddess Māyā-śakti and the Self’s self-veiling. The punctuation must preserve that dependency.

These alternatives are not votes on what to believe. Return to the actual case endings, verb voice and contextual antecedents. If the text permits more than one English formulation, learn what the *Sanskrit* resolves and what the interpretation must supply.

## The Trika turn

Abhinavagupta is not promising miracles on demand. The point is that ultimate freedom can manifest limitation without becoming a second, independent principle.

This is a philosophical reading of the passage within nondual Śaivism. Where a doctrine is supplied by the surrounding text or commentarial tradition, it is not a hidden morpheme in the Sanskrit. Grammar determines what is said; philosophy asks what follows.
