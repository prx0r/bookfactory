## A book about not trusting a beautiful translation

Someone shows you a Sanskrit sentence. “It means the Supreme Self is eternally manifest.” Fine. But what, precisely, means *eternally*? Which noun is the Self? Where is the assertion? Does the verb say “appears,” “becomes,” “shines,” or “is seen”? And if there is no verb at all, who supplied the English “is”?

This book is built for the moment when you stop accepting a translation as authority and begin reconstructing it yourself.

For each of the thirty-six readings you will encounter a historical Sanskrit statement; read it in Devanāgarī; use IAST as a bridge; identify **every visible form**; reverse actual sandhi; distinguish morphological derivation from speculative etymology; reconstruct a literal English sentence; and compare it with a philosophically suggestive alternative. Only then will we discuss the nondual Śaiva interpretation. The grammatical proof always precedes the theological conclusion.

The first selection is two words: **caitanyam ātmā**. By the end, you will read a full verse whose logic depends on masculine dual subjects and a third-person dual passive. That arc cannot be grasped by listening to aphorisms. You must discover *why* the source says what it says.

### Our four promises

**1. The Sanskrit comes first.** Every reading identifies its author or traditional attribution, work and verse number. An extract is visibly identified as an extract, with its source stanza nearby. No modern sentence is passed off as the original.

**2. Every word is accountable.** Form → underlying stem → gender, case and number (or tense, voice and person) → suffix or sandhi → minimal lexical sense. We do not derive every word from a philosophical pun. If the source's internal morphology cannot be settled securely, the entry says so.

**3. Interpretations compete under grammatical constraints.** “Can mean,” “best contextually means,” and “cannot mean by this derivation” are different categories. If a poet writes two identical pronouns with different functions, both functions appear in the notes.

**4. Practice does not manufacture what the text describes.** You may recite, write, remember or contemplate these statements. Do not infer that reciting them forces bodily warmth, light, bliss, or any particular spiritual event. This is a language book with a philosophical heart, not a promise of unusual sensations.

## How to use each lesson

Read the Devanāgarī once, using IAST openly if necessary. Cover the English. Identify the finite verb, if there is one. Underline a noun and its case ending. Reverse the featured sandhi as an equation, and then reconstruct the original phrase. Only after that read the translation laboratory. Choose your own English, *but make every added word accountable*. Finish by writing the selected line in Devanāgarī without looking.

When the grammar seems impossible, begin at the ending. Sanskrit does not generally tell you the subject by putting it first in a sentence: it tells you through **agreement** and **case marking**. In verse 1 there is no expressed verb. In verse 36 the dual forms are the argument. Both become readable when you trust the forms over English word order.

The selections are not arranged chronologically by author. They are arranged by what they teach. *Śiva Sūtras* supplies short nominal equations; Abhinavagupta supplies feminine agreement, instruments and formal definitions; his *Paramārthasāra* supplies metaphysical analogies and longer syntax; Utpaladeva exposes the grammar of devotion; *Spandakārikā* trains you to read clauses about what persists.

## The five things your Devanāgarī reference sheet must teach

**Independent vowel versus vowel sign.** अ is a whole letter; क is *ka* with an inherent short a; कि is *ki* even though the vowel sign is drawn to the left; का is *kā*, and क् suppresses the inherent vowel. The same sound can therefore be printed as an independent vowel or a sign attached to a consonant.

**Consonant clusters.** Sanskrit writes a sequence of consonants in a single visible group: क्त = k + t; ज्ञ = j + ñ; त्र = t + r; द्य = d + y. Do not invent vowels because your English mouth wants a pause.

**Dental versus retroflex.** त (ta) and ट (ṭa), द (da) and ड (ḍa), न (na) and ण (ṇa) are distinct. Sanskrit *anu* (अनु, along/following) is not *aṇu* (अणु, minute/limited individual). This book never makes one a mystical spelling of the other.

**Nasal versus breath sign.** The dot ं is anusvāra, transcribed ṃ. The double-dot shape ः is visarga ḥ. A final म् has a written m consonant with virāma; a final ं has a diacritic. Their pronunciation may converge in context but their written structure does not.

**Short and long vowels matter.** a/ā, i/ī, u/ū are separate phonemes. Sanskrit e and o are long steady vowels, not English sliding diphthongs. Practise writing each IAST word letter by letter until the Devanāgarī feels more direct than the romanisation.

# 01. Consciousness is the Self

**Stage 1: The sentence before the belief**  
**Vasugupta (traditionally attributed) · *Śivasūtra* 1.1**  
**COMPLETE SŪTRA / VERSE**

## The Sanskrit

चैतन्यम् आत्मा

*caitanyam ātmā*

## Before seeing the English

Read the Sanskrit aloud. Cover the translation below. Determine where one word ends, whether any final form has changed through sandhi, and which word is acting as subject or predicate. Ask what the grammar **requires** and what remains open.

**Writing laboratory.** चै = c + ai; त = ta; न्य = n + ya; म् = final m. आत्मा begins with independent आ and uses the conjunct त्म.

| IAST word | Devanāgarī word | Reading units (IAST = script) |
|---|---|---|
| *caitanyam* | चैतन्यम् | cai = चै · ta = त · nya = न्य · m = म् |
| *ātmā* | आत्मा | ā = आ · tmā = त्मा |

These are **orthographic reading units** to help you construct Devanāgarī, not a metrical scansion. When a consonant ends a word without an inherent vowel, notice its virāma. Trace the conjuncts rather than inserting English-style epenthetic vowels.

Write the selected IAST in Devanāgarī using the reference sheet. Check consonants, vowel lengths and the presence of anusvāra (ं), visarga (ः) and virāma (्). It is fine to consult the chart; the point is a repeatable skill, not instant recognition.

## Word-by-word anatomy

| Visible / reconstructed form | Lemma / segmentation | Exact grammatical role | What it can mean |
|---|---|---|---|
| **caitanyam** | caitanya | neuter nominative singular abstract noun, -ya derivative of cetana | consciousness / conscious nature |
| **ātmā** | ātman | masculine nominative singular; stem ātman → nominative ātmā | self, not automatically the ego |

**Reader’s reconstruction:** **caitanyam** = consciousness · **ātmā** = self, not automatically the ego.  Notice how this gives you building blocks rather than an English sentence. Only after identifying the inflections and the omitted or explicit verb should you commit to the literal wording.


## Derivation: reverse it, then rebuild it

1. caitanya + -am → caitanyam (nominative sg)
2. ātman → ātmā (masculine nominative sg)
3. no overt verb: Sanskrit permits a nominal equation with understood asti

**What the derivation demonstrates.** The first line lets the student hear what Sanskrit actually identifies, rather than memorize an English metaphysical slogan.

Before moving on, reverse at least one of the displayed transformations without looking. Distinguish an external sandhi rule (changes at a word boundary) from a suffix or inflection rule (formation of an individual word). They are not interchangeable explanations.

## Translation laboratory

**1. A conservative literal reconstruction:** Consciousness is the self.

**2. Another defensible rendering:** The self is conscious nature; awareness constitutes what is meant by Self.

**3. What the Sanskrit does *not* automatically guarantee:** The Sanskrit establishes an identification, not a Cartesian proof that any individual stream of experience owns the universe. Distinguish caitanya (consciousness) from mere citta (mental contents).

These alternatives are not votes on what to believe. Return to the actual case endings, verb voice and contextual antecedents. If the text permits more than one English formulation, learn what the *Sanskrit* resolves and what the interpretation must supply.

## The Trika turn

Begin with whatever is perceived; ask what knowing itself means. The sūtra points to the subject as consciousness, not to a new object to discover.

This is a philosophical reading of the passage within nondual Śaivism. Where a doctrine is supplied by the surrounding text or commentarial tradition, it is not a hidden morpheme in the Sanskrit. Grammar determines what is said; philosophy asks what follows.

## The case endings that recurred

- Nominative is the likely subject/predicate case, but a Sanskrit sentence can have two nominatives of different genders: *caitanyam ātmā*.
- Accusative often marks the direct object: *vicitrān sṛṣṭisaṃhārān* in lesson 12; *rūpaṃ* in lesson 20.
- Instrumental answers “by/with/in virtue of”: *anayā devyā*, *śaktyā*, *bhaktaiḥ*.
- Genitive marks relation, possession or reference: *tasya*, *sarvasya*, *tattvasya*, *bhaktimatām*.
- Locative means in/at/within, literal or figurative: *tasmin*, *dhīmukure*, *dhītattve*, *parameśvare*.
- Ablative can indicate source, separation, cause or comparison: *jaḍāt*, *māyāparigrahavaśāt*.
- Dual refers to exactly two. The last lesson is unsolvable without *etau*, *-au* and *bhidyete*.

## Three dangers to avoid

1. **Morphology is not cosmology.** The Sanskrit root of a word does not automatically establish a mystical theory about it.
2. **Sandhi is not a suffix.** In *jaḍād* the final d is a context-conditioned voicing of ablative *jaḍāt*; in *jaḍasya* the -sya is a genitive ending, not an external sandhi operation.
3. **A half-verse may not have a verb.** When you supply an English verb, either cite an implied nominal copula, or locate it in the other half of the actual stanza.

## Your one-page reading protocol

1. Circle the **finite verbs**. If there are none, do not invent one until you have checked for a nominal sentence or an incomplete fragment.
2. Underline **subjects and objects** using case and agreement, not English word order.
3. Reverse **external sandhi** separately from **compound formation**.
4. Mark the **lemma and inflection** for each word.
5. Translate conservatively, showing inserted words in square brackets if essential.
6. Propose a philosophically richer rendering, then write down which words and context justify it.
7. Write the line in Devanāgarī from IAST and check every vowel mark.

## Answer key

**01.** ātmā is masculine; caitanyam neuter. A Sanskrit predicate noun need not share gender with its subject.

**02.** Both are nominatives; “is” is supplied in translation as an understood copula.

**03.** udyamo reflects udyamaḥ before the voiced b(h) of bhairavaḥ.

**04.** चित्तं uses anusvāra, while चित्तम् writes explicit m with virāma; they can be equivalent in this phonological position but should not be conflated graphically.

**05.** guruḥ, nominative singular, changes to gurur before upāyaḥ.

**06.** The stem is havis; both śarīram and haviḥ are neuter nominative singulars.

**07.** Predicate nouns can differ in gender; kathā is feminine and japaḥ masculine.

**08.** Underlying śivatulyaḥ produces -o; jāyate is 3rd singular present middle.

**09.** It means possessing bhakti; bhaktimatāṃ is genitive plural, “of devotees.”

**10.** eva + ātmā + īśa; a+ā→ā and ā+ī→e.

**11.** devyā is feminine instrumental singular; anayā is the matching feminine demonstrative instrumental.

**12.** vibhuḥ is nom.sg agent; sṛṣṭisaṃhārān with vicitrān is acc.pl object; yugapad is indeclinable.

**13.** eṣā, parā, devī and the compound ending -utsukā are feminine nominatives; tasya+eva and tasyaiva+eṣā have two contractions.

**14.** nālpam and cādhikam; yasyā is feminine genitive singular yasyāḥ before na.

**15.** -tva, in paricchinnaprakāśatvam. The genitive jaḍasya supplies “of the inert.”

**16.** bodhaḥ with vilakṣaṇaḥ; final t voices to d before the voiced v.

**17.** yat ... tat; a- negates parijñāna, making “non-recognition.”

**18.** yat relates to neuter parijñānam; saḥ is masculine with mokṣaḥ; tattvasya is genitive singular.

**19.** bhoktṛ and dehin; both are masculine nominative singular with stem-specific endings.

**20.** rūpam is acc.sg object; sphaṭikaḥ is nom.sg subject; amalaḥ agrees with sphaṭikaḥ.

**21.** From prakāśate in the first half of the verse; dhīmukure is locative singular.

**22.** śivaśaktipātavimale and dhītattve; bhāti is third-person singular present active.

**23.** jagat is neuter nominative singular subject; tasmin is locative singular; ṣaṭtriṃśadātma qualifies jagat.

**24.** maheśasya is genitive singular. The half-verse lacks a finite verb; its sentence continues.

**25.** advitīyāḥ and tvaddvitīyāḥ, with visarga dropped in the surface; a- negates the idea of a “second.”

**26.** te¹ is gen.sg of the second person; te² nominative plural demonstrative; vidanti is 3pl present.

**27.** -aiḥ marks instrumental plural, “by devotees.” The avagraha marks a lost a from api after -e, while i changes to y before asi.

**28.** asti is present indicative 3rd singular; astu is imperative 3rd singular.

**29.** āhlādaduḥkhamohaiḥ is instrumental plural; tridhā is an indeclinable adverb.

**30.** It agrees with feminine mahāvidyā; yeṣām is genitive plural “of whom.”

**31.** saṃvidaḥ is feminine nom.pl; sukhī and duḥkhī are masculine nom.sg of -in stems.

**32.** -ya produces the potential/passive sense and -aka an agentive sense; asti appears in the second half.

**33.** kṣayiṇī (f.nom.sg) qualifies kāryatā; akṣayam (n.nom.sg) qualifies kartṛtvam.

**34.** All are feminine instrumental singular. They follow different stem classes: -inī, -i, and -ā.

**35.** Voicing of t before b; uḥ→ur before bh; bhavati is 3sg present active of √bhū.

**36.** Third-person dual passive; etau and bandhavimokṣau are both masculine nominative dual. The two are bondage and liberation.

# Afterword — The sentence has become yours

You began with an identification, *caitanyam ātmā*: consciousness, the Self. The English word “is” appeared to be elementary; you learned to ask where it was written. You met *jñānaṃ bandhaḥ* and discovered that a shocking doctrinal statement can be an ordinary nominal construction. You saw a word ending become *o*, and then watched the feminine forms of the Goddess hold together in agreement. You discovered that a pronoun spelled *te* can mean “your” in one place and “they” in another. You learned to distrust an isolated half-verse whenever its verb was in the next half.

This is a different intimacy from memorising a translation. You can still be moved by the words. But the movement no longer depends on someone else's certainty about what they mean. It begins where your eyes meet the signs: *etau* means two; *bhidyete* says “they are divided”; *na* says “not.” The philosophical claim can now be examined, accepted, questioned or refined as a proposition that emerged from Sanskrit, rather than a dramatic caption added by an interpreter.

At the end of the last verse, the two terms—bondage and freedom—do not disappear. The sentence makes a narrower and more difficult claim: **from the standpoint of the supreme reality**, they do not constitute an ultimate division. That condition of standpoint is the thought. Remove it and the verse turns into sentimental confusion. Read it and the thought becomes exact.

The book has not proven the truth of Trika Śaivism. It has given you a better thing than borrowed conviction: the means to hear its claims in the language in which they were made, so that the next judgment can genuinely be yours.
