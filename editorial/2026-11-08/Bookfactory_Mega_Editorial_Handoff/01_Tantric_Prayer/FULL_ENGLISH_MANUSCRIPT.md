## New introduction

This book is for the moments when you have no tidy spiritual feeling. Love is not always gentle. Fear is not always irrational. Anger may know that something is wrong. Wonder does not arrive on command. None needs to be manufactured so that a prayer may begin.

The nine rasas come from a theory of **aesthetic experience**: how emotions, represented and relished in performance or poetry, become available to the sensitive spectator in a special mode. This book borrows their vocabulary as an editorial way of gathering devotional verses. It does **not** claim that an ordinary mood is already an aesthetic rasa, nor that Abhinavagupta invented the ninth category. He offered influential arguments for śānta in a tradition already debating it.

Most of the verses here are from **Utpaladeva**, not Abhinavagupta. They are acts of praise, lament, petition and recognition. Read them as the speaker's address to Śiva. If a phrase comes to memory in an ordinary moment, say it. If a verse is long, read it from the page. Nothing depends on proving that a sensation or emotion has changed.

The experiment is simple: allow the emotion its name, hear the poem, address Śiva, and return to the world with a little more responsibility. A devotional book should not stand between someone and needed care, apology, safety or ordinary action.

## New conclusion

The book does not end with an emotion overcome. It ends where it began: among other people, with a day to live. To say Śiva's name while afraid, thankful, irritated, delighted or grieving is to refuse the false rule that devotion belongs only to a beautiful mood. Whether the feeling changes is not the measure. What matters now is what you do next.
