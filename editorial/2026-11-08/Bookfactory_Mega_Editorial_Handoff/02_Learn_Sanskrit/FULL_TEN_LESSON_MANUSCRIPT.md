# LEARN SANSKRIT WITH ABHINAVAGUPTA
## Volume I — Ten passages from sound to recognition

**Candidate authorial teaching manuscript, not a source-critical Sanskrit edition.** The Sanskrit passages below are copied only from the already-specified bookfactory lesson excerpts and must be collated against a named printed Sanskrit witness before publication. Where the existing lesson used an ellipsis in the source text, it is labelled EXTRACT rather than pretending to print a continuous original verse. Every exercise in English is original; there are no invented Sanskrit utterances attributed to Abhinavagupta.

### How to learn without being bullied by a grammar book

Do not memorise the alphabet first and postpone joy until you are judged ready. On each page, you will see IAST (Roman characters with exact sound marks) and Devanāgarī. Try to write the Devanāgarī from the IAST before unveiling the printed version. Consult the foldout. Check it yourself. By Lesson 7, the script appears first; IAST remains a permanent aid, not a badge of weakness.

Every lesson moves through **witness → sound → separate the words → explain forms → prove junctions → syntax → literal meaning → Trika reading → exercise → solution**. Sound symbolism and Mātṛkā nyāsa are additional traditional subjects. The grammar of a consonant is not established by where a deity is placed upon the body.

## Lesson 1 — Two vowels become one: mamātmā

**Witness:** *Bhairavastava* 2, phrase (EXTRACT; Abhinavagupta attribution).  
**Sanskrit:** ममात्मा — *mamātmā*.  
**Extended context:** त्वं च महेश सदैव ममात्मा — *tvaṃ ca maheśa sadaiva mamātmā* (extract from verse).  
**Literal sense:** “You too, O Maheśa, are always my Self.” The exact scope of *ca* must be checked against the complete stanza.

**The sound.** म is *ma*, because an unwritten short *a* follows म. मा is *mā*, because the ā-sign changes the vowel. मम is *mama*. आत्मा is *ātmā*: an independent long आ, then त् (*t* with virāma), then मा (*mā*). The conjunct त्म combines *t* and *m*. Never confuse *ā* with an emphatic English stress; duration is the contrast.

**Take the words apart.** *mama* is the genitive singular of the first-person pronoun: “my/of me.” *ātmā* is nominative singular masculine of the *ātman* stem: “self.” Those two forms are not one compound merely because they appear without a printed space.

**The exact derivation:**

```
mama + ātmā
mama | ātmā      underlying word boundary
       a + ā -> ā    vowel junction: savarṇadīrgha (Pāṇini 6.1.101)
mamātmā           surface
```

**Syntax.** In the longer phrase *tvaṃ* (you, nominative) and *ātmā* (Self, nominative predicate) are connected by an understood present “are.” *maheśa* is a vocative, an address, not a nominative noun asserting something about the speaker. *sadā* (always) + *eva* (indeed) produces *sadaiva* via vowel junction; it is a **different** junction from the one in *mamātmā*.

**The turn.** The theological claim belongs to the poet's address to Maheśa: the Lord is not simply an external object of worship. The Sanskrit morphology, however, proves only “my Self,” not nondual metaphysics by itself.

**Try it.** Without looking, write मम, आत्मा, ममात्मा. Then reverse *mamātmā* into the two words and name which vowel was lost. Why is *maheśa* an address rather than the object of a verb?

**Answer.** *mama + ātmā*; the final short *a* of *mama* and initial long *ā* contract to *ā*. *maheśa* is the vocative “O Great Lord.”

## Lesson 2 — A bow is a grammatical construction

**Witness:** *Mahopadeśaviṃśatikā* 1, phrase (EXTRACT).  
**Sanskrit:** नमस्ते विश्वमूर्तये — *namaste viśvamūrtaye*.  
**Literal meaning:** “Salutation to you, whose form is the universe.”

**Sound and script.** न *na* is dental, not ण *ṇa*, the retroflex consonant. त *ta* is dental. In स्त (*sta*), the written cluster suppresses the inherent vowel after स्, so the result is *sta*, not *sa-ta*. The long *ū* sign appears in मू (*mū*).

**Separate the words.** *namas* is an indeclinable form for salutation, commonly appearing as *namaḥ* at pause. *te* here means “to you,” an enclitic dative singular of the second-person pronoun. *viśvamūrtaye* is dative singular of *viśvamūrti*, an epithet: “to [one] whose form is the world.” Its internal members are *viśva* (all/world) and *mūrti* (form, embodiment). This is a compound, unlike *mama + ātmā* in Lesson 1.

**Derive the visible surface:**

```
namas + te -> namas te -> namaste    conventional continuous spelling
viśva + mūrti -> viśvamūrti       compound formation
viśvamūrti + dative singular -> viśvamūrtaye
```

The first change is not a magical expansion of *te* into a deity. The syntactic role is given by the dative, not by philosophy.

**Compare** *namo namaḥ* (“salutation, salutation”) in *Mahopadeśaviṃśatikā* 2. *namo* reflects the treatment of underlying final *-as* before a voiced following sound. Do not simply teach that every *-aḥ* always becomes *-o* in all environments; Sanskrit phonological context matters.

**The turn.** One bows to what is seen as the entire world's form, while also addressing a divine “you.” The grammar allows the coexistence of a personal devotional address and a universal epithet; it does not abolish the second person.

**Try it.** Identify two datives. What is the difference between the lexical compound *viśvamūrti* and the external joining of *namas te*? Write ते, मू, तये.

**Answer.** *te* and *viśvamūrtaye* are datives. One indicates the recipient of salutation; the other describes that same recipient. A compound makes a new lexical stem; external sandhi joins adjacent words in pronunciation or spelling.

## Lesson 3 — A location and an action: hṛdi vande

**Witness:** *Bhairavastava* 1, continuous phrase (EXTRACT).  
**Sanskrit:** हृदि वन्दे — *hṛdi vande*.  
**Literal meaning:** “In the heart, I praise/bow [to him].” The object is supplied by the larger verse and must not be invented inside the excerpt.

**Sound and script.** हृ (*hṛ*) is not “hri” with an ordinary short i; ऋ indicates vocalic *ṛ*. The consonant in *vande* is dental द *da*. Read *van-de*, not *va-na-de*. Write हृदि by reading हृ + दि; then वन्दे with its conjunct न्द.

**Morphology.** *hṛd* means heart; *hṛdi* is locative singular, “in the heart.” The locative answers “where?” *vande* is first person singular present middle-voice of √*vand*, “I honour/praise/bow.” The final *-e* is not the infinitive. Verbal voice is a feature of the inflection and must not be translated as if it automatically expresses metaphysical reflexivity.

**Syntax.** An explicit finite verb tells us what the speaker does. The locative gives its setting. An object in the accusative may be present elsewhere in the full verse; the printed excerpt cannot furnish it. Grammar tells us where to look for what is missing.

**The turn.** Worship happens *hṛdi*, in the heart, rather than only at a visible shrine. In Trika this may be read against the theme of consciousness as the locus of experience; that later interpretation is not contained in the locative ending alone.

**Try it.** Point to the case ending in *hṛdi* and person marker in *vande*. Write the two words in Devanāgarī from IAST. Explain why “my heart is God” would be a mistranslation.

**Answer.** *-i* is the locative singular of the relevant stem; *vande* is first-person finite present. The Sanskrit says “in the heart, I praise,” not “the heart is God.”

## Lesson 4 — The same ending, three different acts

**Witness:** *Bhairavastava* 9 (selected contiguous words from the verse; surrounding witness to be collated).  
**Sanskrit:** नृत्यति गायति हृष्यति गाढम् — *nṛtyati gāyati hṛṣyati gāḍham*.  
**Literal meaning:** “Dances, sings, rejoices intensely.” The verse provides the subject; the isolated line need not pretend to be a standalone prayer.

Read the phrase aloud once. Before explanation, circle what repeats: *nṛtya-ti*, *gāya-ti*, *hṛṣya-ti*. Now identify *-ti* as a **third-person singular active present** ending. This is a genuine grammatical pattern, not three related spiritual qualities. The stems differ; the person-number ending repeats.

**Spellings.** नृ uses vocalic *ṛ*; त्य is *tya* with suppressed inherent vowel in त्; ष्य combines retroflex ष with य; गाढम् includes long *ā* and retroflex ḍh. English “hard d” cannot substitute for articulatory accuracy.

**The adverb.** *gāḍham* may function adverbially, “deeply/intensely.” It is not a fourth third-person finite verb. Be willing to identify what a word is *not*.

**Syntax.** In the complete stanza, the verbs describe consciousness, not the personal poet alone; the subject is represented elsewhere as *saṃvid iyam* (“this consciousness”). First check the full witness and agreement before printing a polished theological translation.

**The turn.** The philosophical surprise is that consciousness is portrayed with verbs of ordinary life: dancing and singing. The grammar is mundane and therefore potent. Nothing in *-ti* demands a mystical experience.

**Try it.** Find the three finite verbs. Can *gāḍham* be their common object? Why would “I dance” be wrong when *-ti* is singular third person?

**Answer.** Three finite verbs are *nṛtyati*, *gāyati*, *hṛṣyati*. *gāḍham* is an intensifier. First person would require a different person ending.

## Lesson 5 — Negative commands that do not mean passivity

**Witness:** *Anuttarāṣṭikā* 2, final portion (EXTRACT).  
**Sanskrit:** मा किञ्चित् त्यज मा गृहाण विहर स्वस्थो यथावस्थितः — *mā kiṃcit tyaja mā gṛhāṇa vihara svastho yathāvasthitaḥ*.  
**Translation:** “Abandon nothing; grasp nothing; live at ease, just as you are.”

**New grammatical distinction.** *na* is ordinary negation; *mā* introduces prohibition. *tyaja* is an imperative from √*tyaj*, “abandon.” *gṛhāṇa* is a second-person imperative form from √*grah*, “seize/take.” *vihara* is a second-person imperative from *vi-√hṛ*, “move about, enjoy, live.” The grammar directly addresses a second person; none is a description of the author's past achievement.

*kiṃcit* is an indefinite pronoun/adverbial object, “anything.” In a prohibited command, the phrase *mā kiṃcit tyaja* yields “do not abandon anything.” *svasthaḥ* is masculine nominative singular “established in oneself; well, at ease” and appears as *svastho* before following sounds owing to external sandhi. *yathāvasthitaḥ* is “as situated/as one stands,” again masculine singular agreeing with the implied addressee.

**Build the syntax.** The three imperatives form an intentional series, with *mā* belonging to the first two. “Do not reject; do not grab; live” is not merely a motivational slogan. It appears at the close of a verse that challenges the assumption of bondage and liberation as separate real acquisitions.

**The turn.** The doctrine is not “never leave an abusive situation” or “abandon all obligations.” It challenges metaphysical grasping and rejection. When practical action is needed, act. The verse does not erase ethical discernment.

**Try it.** Mark each imperative. Explain the scope of *mā*. What is the masculine nominative ending in *yathāvasthitaḥ* and why does the surface *svastho* differ from isolated *svasthaḥ*?

**Answer.** *tyaja, gṛhāṇa, vihara*; *mā* negates only the first two commands. *yathāvasthitaḥ* ends in masculine nominative singular *-ḥ*. The *-aḥ* of *svasthaḥ* becomes *-o* in the relevant phonetic context.

## Lesson 6 — A refuge with an explicit object

**Witness:** *Paramārthasāra* 1, excerpt.  
**Sanskrit:** त्वामेव शम्भुं शरणं प्रपद्ये — *tvām eva śambhuṃ śaraṇaṃ prapadye*.  
**Translation:** “I take refuge in you alone, Śambhu.”

**Recognise the case.** *tvām* is accusative singular “you” and *śambhuṃ* is accusative singular “Śambhu.” *śaraṇam* is neuter accusative singular, “refuge”; in idiom with *prapadye*, it can express the refuge sought or entered. The identical *-am* appearance of masculine and neuter accusatives does not mean that all their stems decline alike.

**The verb.** *prapadye* = *pra-* + a verbal formation from √*pad*, present first-person singular middle: “I resort to, approach, take refuge.” Sanskrit can place the verb at the end of the sentence; English need not preserve that order.

**The small word.** *eva* emphasises exclusion or emphasis: “you alone/precisely you.” It is not a separate verb. *tvām eva* may be written consecutively without a further vowel contraction, since *m* before *e* is not an *a + e* boundary.

**The turn.** Refuge is a grammatical act of address by a first-person subject to a second-person Lord. The Trika commentarial reading may later inquire how the apparent two become one. To translate *tvām* as “myself” would erase the very devotional syntax the poet uses.

**Try it.** Underline the three accusatives and one finite verb. Explain why *prapadye* is “I take refuge” rather than “he takes refuge.”

**Answer.** *tvām*, *śambhuṃ*, *śaraṇam*; *prapadye*. First-person singular is marked by the verbal ending.

## Lesson 7 — Exactly two: the Sanskrit dual

**Witness:** *Bodhapañcadaśikā* 3.  
**Sanskrit:** शक्तिश्च शक्तिमद्रूपाद् व्यतिरेकं न वाञ्छति । तादात्म्यमनयोर्नित्यं वह्निदाहिकयोरिव ॥  
**IAST:** *śaktiś ca śaktimadrūpād vyatirekaṃ na vāñchati | tādātmyam anayor nityaṃ vahnidāhikayor iva ||*  
**Working translation:** “Power does not wish to be separate from the form of its possessor; the identity of these two is perpetual, like that of fire and its burning power.”

**What changes?** Sanskrit has singular, dual and plural. *anayor* is the surface form of *anayoḥ*, a genitive/locative **dual** of the demonstrative *idam*, “of/in these two.” *vahnidāhikayor* similarly reflects a **dual** form *vahnidāhikayoḥ* joined before *iva*. The final *r* is produced by external sandhi from underlying *-ḥ* in these environments; the dual case is not formed by sandhi itself. This corrects an error in the existing machine lesson.

**Grammar through the clause.** *śaktiḥ* is the feminine nominative singular subject of *vāñchati* (“desires”). *na* negates that finite verb. *vyatirekam* is masculine accusative singular, “separation/distinction.” *śaktimadrūpāt* is ablative singular, “from the form of the bearer of power.” *tādātmyam* names identity; *iva* marks comparison, not identity of every physical property.

**The first sandhi:** *śaktiḥ + ca → śaktiś ca*; the visarga before a palatal stop becomes ś in the standard sandhi pattern. *śaktimat + rūpa* also deserves exact internal morphophonemic explanation from a verified grammatical analysis; do not derive it by mere English analogy.

**The turn.** The verse argues through the relation of heat and fire: power is neither an externally added instrument nor an unrelated second substance. The analogy supports the inseparability of Śiva and Śakti without exhausting their meaning.

**Try it.** Why is *anayor* not a plural? What does *iva* do? Reconstruct *anayor nityaṃ* and *vahnidāhikayor iva* into their pre-sandhi endings.

**Answer.** *-yoḥ* is genitive/locative dual; *iva* says “as/like.” Underlying forms: *anayoḥ nityam* and *vahnidāhikayoḥ iva*.

## Lesson 8 — An instrumental after “enough”

**Witness:** *Mahopadeśaviṃśatikā* 8, first half only.  
**Sanskrit:** अलं भेदानुकथया त्वद्भक्तिरसचर्वणात् ।  
**IAST:** *alaṃ bhedānukathayā tvadbhaktirasacarvaṇāt*.  
**Working sense:** “Enough of discussing difference—because of savouring the flavour of devotion to You.”

**The construction.** *alaṃ* is an indeclinable meaning “enough”; combined with an instrumental it can express “enough of …”. *bhedānukathayā* is feminine instrumental singular: *bheda* (difference) + *anukathā* (recounting/discourse) → compound stem *bhedānukathā* → instrumental *bhedānukathayā*. Two separate operations occur: vowel sandhi at *bheda + anukathā* and nominal inflection at the end.

**Make the proof:**

```
bheda + anukathā        a + a -> ā       [compound-internal junction]
bhedānukathā           feminine ā-stem
bhedānukathā + INS.SG  -> bhedānukathayā [inflection, not another sandhi]
```

**A similar-looking trap.** अनु *anu* is not अणु *aṇu*. The first is a prefix or preposition with following/repetition senses; the second denotes smallness and is used in Trika for the limited individual. Dental *n* and retroflex *ṇ* are distinct phonemes. This is an especially good moment to find both on your alphabet reference sheet.

**The causal phrase.** *tvad-bhakti-rasa-carvaṇa* is a compound “savouring of the taste of devotion to you”; *-āt* marks ablative singular in *carvaṇāt*, capable of expressing cause/source, “because of/from.” The word is not a finite verb: there is **no finite verb** in this extract. That is not an error; the thought continues in the next half.

**The turn.** Abhinavagupta does not say difference is disproved by grammatical parsing. He says the relish of devotion makes repeated discussion of difference unnecessary. This is why a whole verse matters: the next two lines show an even stranger reserve about saying “everything is one.”

**Try it.** Classify *-ayā* and *-āt*. Which operation produces the long ā in *bhedānukathā*? Why is *anu* not the Trika limited individual?

**Answer.** Feminine instrumental singular; ablative singular. *a + a → ā*. The limited individual is *aṇu* with retroflex ṇ.

## Lesson 9 — The same verse, now complete

**Witness:** *Mahopadeśaviṃśatikā* 8, all four quarters.  
**Sanskrit:** अलं भेदानुकथया त्वद्भक्तिरसचर्वणात् । सर्वमेकमिदं शान्तमिति वक्तुं च लज्जते ॥  
**IAST:** *alaṃ bhedānukathayā tvadbhaktirasacarvaṇāt | sarvam ekam idaṃ śāntam iti vaktuṃ ca lajjate ||*  
**Working translation:** “Enough of discourse about difference: through savouring the rasa of devotion to you, one is even ashamed to say, ‘All this is one, peaceful.’”

**First read the sentence aloud**, counting roughly eight metrical syllables per quarter. The caesura may be audible; it does not determine the syntactic word boundaries. Mark the first half you know from Lesson 8.

**Now the second half.** These are **separate words**, not one compound:

```
sarvam | ekam | idam | śāntam | iti | vaktum | ca | lajjate
all    | one  | this  | at peace | thus | to say | even/and | is ashamed
```

*Sarvam*, *ekam*, *idam* and *śāntam* are neuter singular forms in this construction. They express the proposition quoted by *iti*: “all this is one, peaceful.” A translation must not misdescribe the group as a long *samāsa*. By contrast, *tvadbhaktirasacarvaṇa* actually is a compound.

**Sandhi versus spacing.** In an uninterrupted copy, *sarvam ekam idaṃ* may appear as *sarvamekamidaṃ*. This concatenation does not turn the words into a compound. Likewise *śāntam iti* is often printed *śāntamiti*. The underlying boundary is **śāntam + iti**, not *śānta + iti*. Ending *-m* belongs to the inflected adjective and must not be discarded.

**The verb.** *vaktum* is an infinitive (“to say”), related to √*vac*, not a present tense or a routine vowel-sandhi product. *lajjate* is third-person singular present middle voice, “is ashamed/feels diffident.” Its subject is unexpressed, supplied by context. A singular finite verb can have a generic subject in a translation (“one hesitates/is ashamed”).

**The interpretive surprise.** One expects the nondualist to proclaim “all is one.” Instead the poem depicts devotion making even that proclamation seem inadequate. This does not license the grammatically false translation “the world is an illusion”; the quote explicitly says *sarvam ekam idaṃ śāntam*. The reluctance concerns **speaking** the claim, the infinitive *vaktum* governed by *lajjate*.

**Try it.** Give the case/number of *bhedānukathayā*, *carvaṇāt* and *śāntam*; identify the finite verb; explain the role of *iti*. Separate *sarvamekamidaṃ śāntamiti* without inventing a compound.

**Answer.** Instrumental feminine singular; ablative singular; neuter nominative/accusative singular (contextual predicate/quotation); *lajjate*; *iti* closes the quoted proposition; *sarvam | ekam | idam | śāntam | iti*.

## Lesson 10 — A philosophical argument in prose

**Witness:** *Tantrasāra*, Āhnika 1, opening argument (EXTRACT). **Current project labels the L0 witness machine-proposed. Publication hold until first-hand collation.**  
**Sanskrit as presently transcribed:** इह ज्ञानं मोक्षकारणं बन्धनिमित्तस्य अज्ञानस्य विरोधकत्वात् — *iha jñānaṃ mokṣakāraṇaṃ bandhanimittasya ajñānasya virodhakatvāt*.  
**Working translation:** “Here, knowledge is the cause of liberation, because it is opposed to ignorance, the cause of bondage.”

**What is different from poetry?** There is no metre organising four quarters, and no explicit finite verb in this excerpt. Sanskrit philosophical prose often uses **nominal clauses**: *jñānam* (knowledge, neuter nominative singular) is identified with *mokṣakāraṇam* (cause of liberation, neuter nominative singular). English normally supplies “is.”

**Break the argument into roles:**

```
iha                 'here, in this account'           indeclinable
jñānam              'knowledge'                       neuter nominative singular
mokṣa-kāraṇam       'cause of liberation'             neuter nominative predicate
bandha-nimittasya   'of the cause of bondage'          genitive singular
ajñānasya           'of ignorance'                     genitive singular, appositional
virodhakatvāt       'on account of opposedness'        ablative singular of abstract -tva
```

**Why -tvāt?** The abstract noun *virodhakatva* (“being opposed/oppositional character”) ends in an ablative singular *-āt*: the ablative can give a reason. *Ajñāna* is “not-knowing/ignorance,” but the prefix *a-* may create different kinds of semantic negation depending on formation; do not pretend a complete theory of ignorance follows from splitting an initial *a*.

**The logic.** The form supplies a **because-clause**, not a proof of its premise. The writer argues that knowledge releases because it contradicts ignorance. A reader may now ask: what kind of ignorance? Is it an incorrect belief, a more fundamental contraction, or both? This question belongs to the surrounding Tantrasāra discussion, and the answer must be sourced from the **next actual prose**, not invented from metaphysical enthusiasm.

**The turn.** The student began with two vowels fusing in *mamātmā*. Now a philosophical proposition is not an opaque miracle: it is a sentence with a subject, nominal predicate, genitive specification and ablative reason. That is real progress even if the doctrine remains open to argument.

**Try it.** Write one grammatical reason for translating “because.” Identify the genitive pair, the nominal predicate, and one reason why the passage cannot be called a verse. Separate morphology from philosophical truth.

**Answer.** Ablative *-tvāt* can indicate cause; *bandhanimittasya ajñānasya* specify ignorance as the cause of bondage; *mokṣakāraṇam* predicates “cause of liberation” of *jñānam*. It is prose, not a metrical stanza. Its claim must still be evaluated philosophically.

## Final examination — real reading, not remembered slogans

Supply an **unseen** two-line Abhinavagupta passage only after obtaining a signed source. The learner must: (1) render its IAST in Devanāgarī using the sheet, (2) reverse all identifiable external sandhi, (3) classify each familiar declensional or verbal form, (4) mark literal words versus inserted English copulas, (5) provide a cautious provisional translation, and (6) distinguish historical text from Trika interpretation. An instructor should score evidence of method, not devotional vocabulary. Do not claim mastery of every Sanskrit case or advanced compounds after ten lessons.
